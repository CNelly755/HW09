# 04 Counterevidence and the case against my own recommendation

The process asks for a chatbot to try to refute the sources. I did not have a separate chatbot, so I tried to argue against my own paper here. Cameron should paste the paper into another model and add its objections below.

## Objection 1: the VOC's price was high in the 1720s, so a 1725 buyer might have done well

Koot says price records were set in the 1720s. If you bought in January 1725 and the price rose, you could have earned a capital gain even with a 3.4 percent yield. I do not have the price path from 1725 to 1745. My recommendation to prefer the English Company rests on dividend and profit evidence, not on a measured return. A better test is to compare the actual 1725 buy price and the 1745 sell price for both firms in Castaing and the Amsterdamsche Courant. I have not done it.

Weight: high. This is the biggest gap in the paper.

## Objection 2: the VOC's bond-like yield is a virtue, not a flaw

A steady 3 to 4 percent paid by a firm with enormous assets could be rational for a cautious investor. Koot's "like bonds" can be read as stability. The reply is that Koot also says the firm was paying out more than it earned, which means the dividend was not sustainable. But it was sustained for decades, and a 1725 investor who sold in 1745 would never have seen it fail.

Weight: medium.

## Objection 3: the English Company's trouble after 1740 might not have hit the price by early 1745

The French threat in Robins starts in 1740, but the Company's dividend might have stayed high into the mid-1740s. I do not know. The loan of 1744 was paid for a monopoly extension, which could count as good for shareholders because it secured the Company's future. If the price rose after 1744, selling in early 1745 would give up gains.

Weight: high. The letter admits this and sends the reader to Castaing.

## Objection 4: the war in India may have helped the English

The French took Madras in 1746, but the Company recovered it under the 1748 peace, and the 1750s are the period in which the English did much better in India. A holder to 1750 may have done well. My notes cannot rule that out. Robins says dividends fell into the early 1750s, which supports my view, but that is one lecture.

Weight: medium.

## Objection 5: the ethical comparison treats the Batavia killings as a company act

Valckenier's own letter says 10,000 were killed. But Stellwagen also records that the Seventeen annulled the arrests of the councillors and later arrested Valckenier. A defender of the VOC would say the Company in the Netherlands did not order the killing and later punished the Governor-General. A critic would say it first sided with him, and punished him only after two years. Both readings are in Stellwagen. The letter gives both facts but leans on the shareholder profiting from the Company.

Weight: medium. The letter's claim is modest, though: that the shareholder is paid by the same firm.

## Objection 6: "the English Company is the smaller moral cost" is a comparison I cannot fully support

For the English side I have only the Bennett abstract (slavery at St Helena and Bencoolen) and the Robins remark about the Royal African Company. For the Dutch side I have a documented killing of at least 10,000 people in 1740, plus Banda. The English record in India in these years I did not read at all. The comparison is therefore one-sided because of the evidence I had, not necessarily because of the history. The letter's final claim is worded as "the smaller moral cost" and should be softened if Cameron finds English atrocities in the window.

Weight: high.

## Objection 7: sources

Only three of the nine period sources were read, and one of the three is an 1895 article. An examiner will notice that the letter is mostly built on modern secondary sources. The honest fix is to read the other period sources. No wording can cure this.

## Things that would change my recommendation

- A price series showing the English Company's stock falling sharply before 1745, which would move the sale earlier.
- A price series showing the VOC outperforming the English Company from 1725 to 1745, which would reverse the purchase advice.
- Evidence of large EIC dividend cuts before 1745, which would support the sell date.
- Evidence of EIC violence in the window comparable to Batavia, which would remove the ethical preference.
